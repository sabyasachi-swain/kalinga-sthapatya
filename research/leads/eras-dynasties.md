# **Architectural and Dynastic History of Orissan Temples (2nd Century BCE – 16th Century CE)**

The architectural heritage of Odisha, historically referred to as Kalinga, Utkala, or Odra, represents one of the most continuous and well-documented traditions of stone temple construction in the Indian subcontinent. The evolution of this regional style—often categorized under the broader Northern or Indo-Aryan architectural umbrella—spans from the early rock-cut ascetic dwellings of the second century BCE to the towering structural monuments of the sixteenth century CE. This report synthesizes the dynastic histories, chronological frameworks, religious affiliations, and specific architectural contributions of the major ruling houses of ancient and medieval Odisha: the Mahameghavahana, Shailodbhava, Bhauma-Kara, Somavamshi, Eastern Ganga, and Suryavamshi Gajapati dynasties.  
The analysis relies strictly on accessible epigraphical evidence, archaeological survey reports, and established scholarly texts. The reconstruction of this history requires careful navigation of epigraphic records (such as copper-plate grants and stone inscriptions) alongside traditional chronicles (such as the Madala Panji), which often present conflicting narratives regarding dates, patronage, and capital cities.

## **Scholarly Periodization of Orissan Architecture**

The categorization of Orissan temple architecture into distinct chronological or stylistic phases has been a subject of continuous refinement among historians and archaeologists. Establishing an absolute chronology is frequently precluded by the nature of the data available, as many temples lack foundation inscriptions and must be dated through comparative stylistic and iconographic analysis1. The following schemes represent the primary frameworks utilized by foundational scholars in the field.

### **Percy Brown's Classification**

Percy Brown, in his seminal work *Indian Architecture (Buddhist and Hindu Periods)* (D. B. Taraporevala Sons, 1959, 4th Edition), broadly groups the mature structural temples of the region under a single chronological bracket in his chapter headings. While later secondary literature sometimes extrapolates a strict "Early, Middle, and Later" division from his work, the accessible text of his overarching classification categorizes the mature regional development strictly as: "THE NORTHERN, OR INDO-ARYAN STYLE ORISSA (A.D. 800 to 1250)"2. He further identifies an earlier phase of regional activity, specifically the "ROCK ARCHITECTURE OF ORISSA (2nd CENTURY B.C.)"3, isolating the early cave architecture from the later structural Hindu temples.

### **K. C. Panigrahi's Classification**

Dr. Krishna Chandra Panigrahi, in his exhaustive study *Archaeological Remains at Bhubaneswar* (Orient Longmans, 1961), eschews rigid numerical or strictly dynastic era labels in favor of stylistic associations. Panigrahi explicitly states that his main attempt is "to assign the numerous monuments of Bhubaneswar to the broad political epochs, but not to fix their accurate dates, which in many cases is precluded by the nature of the data available for the purpose"1. Consequently, he organizes the architectural history of the region by grouping monuments that share distinct architectural and iconographical peculiarities. His formal classification scheme, as listed in Chapter IX of his text, is explicitly titled: "Chronology of the Temples: Cognate Groups"1. This methodology allows for temples without secure foundation inscriptions to be dated relative to securely dated anchor monuments.

### **Thomas E. Donaldson's Classification**

Thomas E. Donaldson, in his multi-volume *Hindu Temple Art of Orissa* (E. J. Brill, 1985–1987), analyzes the micro-evolution of temple elements. While rigid, capitalized era labels such as "Formative / Transitional / Mature" are often misattributed as an absolute tripartite rule in tertiary sources, Donaldson's actual textual analysis utilizes descriptive phases to track the evolution of the Orissan style. The accessible texts referencing his work note that the "Somavamsi period witnessed a formative phase in the temple architecture"4. Furthermore, scholarly summaries of his and other contemporary analyses note that "the evolution of the Orissan style can be divided into four phases viz, early, middle, phase of maturity and phase of..."6, indicating a highly nuanced, multi-stage developmental timeline rather than a simple three-part nomenclature.

The chronological position of the Bhauma-Karas has been meticulously established through epigraphic discoveries. As noted by K.C. Panigrahi, "According to the revised chronology the Bhauma-Karas and Somavamsis ruled in Orissa from a.d. 736 to c. a.d. 931 and from c. a.d. 931 to c. a.d. 1110 respectively"1. Operating from their capital at Guhesvarapataka11, the Bhauma-Kara rulers exhibited a complex religious identity. In their official inscriptions, the kings utilized the title "Paramasaugata," indicating they were devout Buddhists11. However, this period witnessed the flourishing of esoteric Sakta and Saiva cults, resulting in the construction of unique structural monuments such as the Vaital Deul at Bhubaneswar. The Vaital Deul is an architectural anomaly in the region, representing a rare example of the *khakhara* order, which features a semi-cylindrical vaulted roof derived from earlier timber architecture13. This temple, dedicated to the fearsome goddess Chamunda, is attributed by scholars to the Bhauma-Kara period based on its stylistic and iconographic program14. For educational contexts, a highly engaging fact is that the Bhauma-Kara dynasty was distinguished by the successive rule of several powerful queens, such as Tribhuvana Mahadevi I and Prithvi Mahadevi, who ruled in their own right15.

The Somavamshi period, spanning c. A.D. 931 to c. A.D. 11101, represents the golden age of Saiva architecture in Bhubaneswar. Epigraphic and architectural evidence demonstrates that the "Somavamsi period witnessed a formative phase in the temple architecture"4. While traditional temple chronicles like the Madala Panji refer to these rulers as the Kesari (Lion) dynasty holding court at Jajapur and building a palace at Chaudwar16, epigraphic evidence shows the dynasty originally migrated from Dakshina Kosala and eventually oversaw the "Transfer of the Somavamsi Capital to the coastal region"15.  
The monumental achievement of this era is the construction of massive stone temples, most notably the great Lingaraja temple and the Brahmesvara temple. The chronology of this era is significantly aided by a vital epigraphic record: the Brahmesvara temple inscription. This inscription securely links the construction of the temple to "Kolavati Devi, the mother of Maharajadhiraja Udyoto Kesari"17, providing an absolute chronological anchor in an otherwise stylistically dated landscape.

The Eastern Ganga dynasty, originally a lesser-known branch from northern Andhradesa, achieved paramountcy in Odisha under the leadership of Anantavarman Chodagangadeva, who defeated the Somavamshi kings and "ruled from 1074-1154 approximately"18. The capital of this vast empire was firmly established at "Varanasi-kataka or Abhinava Varanasi. This town is identified with the modern Cuttack"18.  
The Eastern Gangas are responsible for two of the most recognizable monuments in India. First, the Jagannatha temple at Puri. Second, the spectacular Sun Temple at Konark. Built "towards the middle of the thirteenth century by the Eastern Ganga king, Narasimhadeva, it is unrivalled for its unique architecture and exquisite sculptures"19. For educational purposes, the most engaging feature of Konark is its conceptual design: it was "conceived in the form of a mammoth, twelve-wheeled chariot drawn by seven galloping horses"19. It is critical to note that the primary soaring tower (*rekha deul*) of the Konark temple has completely collapsed. As documented by Debala Mitra, "Although the tower of the sanctuary collapsed long ago, the Konarak Sun Temple is still a monument of colossal magnificence. What remains of the Sun Temple is the porch or jagamohana, which originally shared a platform with the sanctuary, and the pillared bhoga-mandapa in front"19.

The Suryavamshi Gajapati dynasty emerged under the leadership of "Gajapati Kapilesvara Deva (1435-1436 A.D.)"20. ## **Historiographical Conflicts**

The study of Orissan temple architecture is frequently complicated by contradictions between scholarly interpretations, epigraphic evidence, and traditional chronicles.

### **The Foundation of the Jagannatha Temple**

A primary point of conflict concerns the exact origins of the Jagannatha temple at Puri. Traditional accounts often push the foundation dates backward to align with earlier legendary kings, while epigraphists rely strictly on copper-plate grants. Even within single modern sources summarizing the history, the conflict is apparent.

* **View A (Eastern Ganga Foundation):** The temple was entirely a creation of the Eastern Ganga dynasty. Quote: "...the present temple was built by the founder of the Ganga dynasty in Orissa, Ananta Varman Choda Gangadeva, who ruled from 1074-1154 approximately"18.  
* **View B (Somavamshi Antecedents):** The temple was a reconstruction over an older Somavamshi structure. Quote: "The Jagannatha temple was built on the ruins of an earlier temple built by the Keshari Emperor, Yayati Keshari and seems to have been erected in its present form near about 1000 A.D."18. This disagreement highlights the tension between recognizing the undeniable massive architectural contribution of Chodagangadeva and acknowledging the persistent local traditions (such as those recorded in the Madala Panji) that attribute early foundations to Yayati Kesari.

### **The Capitals of the Somavamshi Dynasty**

A similar conflict exists regarding the capital cities of the Somavamshi kings.

* **View A (Traditional Northern Capitals):** Chronicles suggest the court was fixed primarily in the north. Quote: "His Court was held at Jajapur where he built a palace (Nour) and Castle, called Chuudwar, or Mansion with four gates"16.  
* **View B (Epigraphic Coastal Migration):** Epigraphists mapping the movement of the dynasty from Dakshina Kosala note a later, strategic "Transfer of the Somavamsi Capital to the coastal region"15.
