# **The Parasuramesvara Temple of Bhubaneswar: A Comprehensive Architectural and Historical Analysis**

The Parasuramesvara Temple, located in Bhubaneswar, stands as a critical monument in the historiography of early Orissan (Kalinga) temple architecture. Serving as a crucial transition point in both structural engineering and iconographic development, the temple has been the subject of extensive scholarly debate. This report provides an exhaustive, evidence-based analysis of the temple's physical attributes, historical context, and architectural lineage, derived exclusively from recognized archaeological and epigraphic sources.

## **1\. Date of Construction and Chronological Debates**

The precise date of the Parasuramesvara temple's construction remains one of the most vigorously debated subjects in Orissan archaeology, with scholars divided between stylistic comparanda and epigraphic evidence. The historiographical conflict largely centers on whether the temple belongs to the late Gupta period or a later 8th-century epoch.  
Scholars prioritizing architectural motifs suggest an earlier chronology. Analyzing the structural ornamentation, K.C. Panigrahi addresses the earlier views of R.D. Banerji, noting that the temple possesses distinct, archaic stylistic markers. Panigrahi observes that "If the Chaitya windows on the Parasuramesvara are in 'the pure early Gupta style,' the date of the temple should not be considered far removed from the Gupta Age"1. This assessment challenges chronologies that place the temple centuries after the collapse of the Gupta empire, suggesting a direct continuity of northern classical aesthetics into the Kalinga region.  
Conversely, alternative assessments rely on explicit epigraphic records to anchor the temple in a later era. R.D. Banerji points to specific lithic evidence, asserting that the structure is "described as the oldest temple in Orissa, dating no earlier than the 8th century A.D. according to the inscription on its Navagraha-slab"2.  
Further complicating this timeline is relative dating based on palaeographical evolution. When comparing the Parasuramesvara to neighboring shrines, Panigrahi employs a meticulous analysis of script development. He argues that the structural timeline must follow the evolution of the script, stating: "Palaeographical features show that the letter forms for ja, ha, and la in the Parasuramesvara inscriptions are more developed than those on the Satrughnesvara, indicating that Satrughnesvara is earlier (latter half of the 6th century A.D.) than Parasuramesvara"1. This palaeographical sequence definitively places Parasuramesvara later than Satrughnesvara, establishing a relative regional chronology even as absolute dates remain contested.

## **2\. Patronage and Dynastic Affiliation**

While the specific monarch or overarching dynasty responsible for commissioning the Parasuramesvara temple is not explicitly named in the available epigraphic record, the inscriptions provide critical insight into the religious sectarian patronage that funded and managed the site.  
The temple functioned under the purview of ascetics, specifically those of the Pasupata sect. The most revealing evidence of patronage comes from a direct lithic record on the temple itself. Panigrahi documents this, noting that "An inscription on the Parasuramesvara temple mentions a donor named Pramada who styles himself as an acharya (a title given to Pasupata teachers such as Udita in the Mathura Pillar inscription of Chandragupta II), indicating that Pramada was likely a Pasupata teacher"1. The presence of a high-ranking Pasupata *acharya* operating as a principal donor strongly implies that the temple was an active institutional center for Pasupata Saivism. This specific sectarian alignment helps explain the iconographic choices present on the exterior walls, which heavily favor ascetic theology.

## **3\. Deity and Religious Affiliation**

The Parasuramesvara temple is unequivocally a Saiva sanctuary, a fact corroborated by the extensive iconographic program dominating its exterior walls. The thematic consistency of the relief carvings centers on the life, manifestations, and ascetic lineage of Siva.  
The principal indicators of its Saivite dedication include depictions of the supreme deity alongside his consort, as well as images of key sectarian founders. Panigrahi notes the presence of a "rectangular niche showing Hara and Parvati seated side-by-side"1. Furthermore, the temple explicitly honors the Pasupata sect's founder, Lakulisa. The depiction of Lakulisa is rendered with distinct iconographic traits, including an erect *membrum virile*, which serves as a definitive marker "indisputably indicating its Saivite origin"1. The current status of the temple regarding active worship (whether it operates as a "living temple") is not documented in the available archaeological surveys.

## **4\. Architectural Typology and Plan**

The spatial and geometric layout of the Parasuramesvara temple provides vital data regarding the formative stages of Orissan temple planning. Unlike later structures that featured highly complex, multi-faceted ground plans (such as *pancharatha* or *saptaratha*), this temple adheres to a more foundational geometry.  
Scholars analyzing the main sanctuary (vimana) observe that the structure is based on a distinct cross-shaped geometric grid. R.D. Banerji outlines the fundamental layout, stating that "Its Vimana is regularly cruciform or Ratha in plan"2. The vertical projections, or *pagas*, which modulate the exterior walls and break the monotony of the square base, are relatively subdued at this early architectural stage. Panigrahi observes that these pagas "appear as flat projections or shallow buttresses," indicating a transitional phase in the evolution of wall articulation1. The towering superstructure (sikhara) also reflects this early stage of development; rather than a soaring verticality, it possesses a profile "that curves gradually towards the top"2.  
While the exact ratha-classification (whether specifically *triratha* or *pancharatha*) is highly sought after by architectural historians, the accepted source texts available limit the description to the general term "Ratha in plan" without enumerating the precise number of vertical offsets.

## **5\. Component Structures: Vimana and Jagamohana**

The architectural composition of the Parasuramesvara temple is bipartite, consisting solely of the main sanctuary tower (the *vimana* or *deul*) and the frontal assembly hall (the *jagamohana*). Later Orissan complexes often included a dance hall (*natamandira*) and an offering hall (*bhogamandapa*), but these are absent here, reflecting the simpler ritual requirements of the era.  
The visual rhythm of the vimana is heavily dependent on the intricate carvings that adorn its superstructure. The *sikhara* is not a plain curvilinear surface; rather, it is heavily textured with specific architectural elements. Panigrahi details these components, recording that "Its sikhara is decorated with Chaitya arches, bhumi-amalakas, shallow niches, and medallions (including a large medallion in the raha-pagas)"1. The inclusion of *bhumi-amalakas* (ribbed, circular stones marking the ascending levels of the tower) is a hallmark of the northern *Nagara* style.  
Attached to the vimana is the *jagamohana*, which represents a critical study in early hall design. Unlike the stepped, pyramidal (*pidha*) roofs of the mature Orissan period, this hall utilizes a different roofing strategy. Panigrahi describes it as "a rectangular structure with a clerestory and massive eaves sculptured with battle scenes and episodes from Siva's life"1. The use of a clerestory allowed indirect light to enter the interior, an essential feature given the massive, enclosing nature of stone architecture.

## **6\. Dimensions and Spatial Layout**

Establishing the exact dimensions of the Parasuramesvara temple reveals conflicts in the historical survey data, highlighting the difficulties inherent in early 20th-century monument documentation. Measurements taken by different scholars offer varying figures for the scale of the structure.  
Regarding the overall elevation of the sanctuary, R.D. Banerji quotes an earlier architectural historian's survey, stating: "Mano Mohan Ganguly the height of the Vimana is 60 ft."3. This figure emphasizes the imposing verticality of the main tower relative to its surroundings.  
Conversely, Percy Brown provides a metric focused on the depth and internal arrangement of the structure, recording a measurement of "...feet across and 44 feet deep having three aisles of three pillars"5. This 44-foot measurement explicitly describes the internal spatial volume and pillar arrangement rather than the exterior height.  
The dimensions of the frontal hall are precisely documented in later decorative art surveys. R.P. Mohapatra's analysis of the *jagamohana* outlines its rectangular footprint and specific fenestration, noting: "The structure ( 29'4" \>\< 22'6" ) is fitted with two doors, one to the south and the other to the west and four windows"6. This dual-door configuration was highly unusual and demonstrates experimentation in traffic flow and ritual access within the early Kalinga style. (Note: Specific ASI measured drawings corroborating an IGNCA height of 12.80 m are absent from the accepted literature).

## **8\. Materials and Quarry Sources**

While the vast majority of the temple is constructed using dry masonry (the standard technique for Orissan temples, relying on gravity and weight rather than mortar), specific architectural features highlight the integration of specialized, high-quality lithic materials to achieve desired aesthetic effects.  
The most notable use of specialized stone occurs in the fenestration of the *jagamohana*. R.D. Banerji observes that to allow light into the hall, "The side openings were filled towards the bottom with a jali (lattice) of blue chlorite, topped by a frieze of four miniature temple Sikharas"2. The selection of blue chlorite for the *jali* screens is highly significant; chlorite is a fine-grained, relatively soft stone when freshly quarried, allowing for the incredibly delicate, intricate carving required for lattice windows, which then hardens upon exposure to air. The specific quarry sources for this chlorite, or for the primary sandstone of the walls, are not detailed in the texts.

## **9\. Construction and Structural Engineering**

The engineering of the Parasuramesvara temple, particularly its *jagamohana*, reveals a fascinating narrative of architectural experimentation, structural failure, and subsequent adaptive masonry. The methods used here illustrate the trial-and-error process inherent in the evolution of early Indian structural temples.  
The roof of the frontal hall was originally engineered to span a wide space without the use of true arches. Banerji points out that "The roof of the Jagamohana was built on the cantilever principle"2. To support this immense, overhanging weight, the load was transferred down to an internal framework. The structure was originally "supported on twelve large round pillars arranged in a hollow square"2.  
This initial design intended for the hall to be highly permeable to light and air. Banerji deduces that the hall was originally "open on all sides"2. However, the ambitious engineering ultimately proved unstable. The massive stone lintels resting on the pillars could not sustain the tension and sheer forces over time. Banerji chronicles this structural evolution: "Later, after the lintels on all sides gave way, the gaps between pillars were filled in with ashlar masonry, leaving four openings"2. The addition of ashlar masonry transformed the airy, open pavilion into a dark, enclosed chamber, fundamentally altering the phenomenological experience of the interior space in a desperate bid to prevent total structural collapse. (Details regarding iron cramps, dowels, or construction ramps are absent from the sources).

## **10\. Special Features and Iconography**

The exterior walls of the Parasuramesvara temple function as a vast, lithic canvas, displaying a complex and dynamic iconographic program that would captivate any observer. The sculptures operate not merely as decoration, but as a visual theology, weaving narrative episodes and divine manifestations into the very fabric of the building.  
The western facade contains highly dynamic divine imagery. Panigrahi details a magnificent cosmic dance scene, noting that the wall "Features a ten-handed Nataraja within a medallion inside an ornate Chaitya arch"1. This multi-armed depiction of Siva's dance represents the cyclical nature of creation and destruction. Below this, the cosmic order is further emphasized by solar imagery, featuring an "image of Surya driven by Aruna with seven horses, accompanied by Usa and Pratyusa discharging arrows"1.  
Furthermore, the temple provides a rare visual record of ancient ascetic practices. On the western facade of the *jagamohana*, an explicit "scene of linga-worship depicting ascetics with matted hair and loin-cloths" is carved in bas-relief, offering a glimpse into the actual rituals performed by the Pasupata monks who operated the site1. Additionally, the pierced stone windows are not merely functional; they are highly artistic, with one specifically identified as a "Grill with Dancing Figures," marrying architectural necessity with vibrant sculptural life1.

## **11\. Geographic Context and Site Purpose**

The available scholarly texts do not provide explicitly sourced statements regarding why this specific geographical site was chosen, the patron's exact socio-political purpose for the foundation, or the presence of earlier, pre-existing shrines precisely on this spot.

## **12\. Stylistic Influences and Architectural Lineage**

The Parasuramesvara temple does not exist in an architectural vacuum; it is a critical node in a broader pan-Indian structural dialogue. It both inherits stylistic traits from earlier classical traditions and bequeaths its own innovations to subsequent Orissan masterworks.  
Looking to its predecessors, the temple exhibits a clear debt to the architectural vocabulary of the Gupta and post-Gupta eras of Central and Western India. Banerji points out that the curvature of the main tower is not isolated, but rather curves in a manner akin to the "Dasavatara temple at Deogadh and the Durga temple at Aihole," placing it within an early, pan-regional stylistic continuum2. Furthermore, the specific design of the Chaitya window medallions is noted as being "exactly similar to those on the Saiva temple at Bhumara," cementing its connection to 6th-century central Indian aesthetics1.  
Conversely, the Parasuramesvara temple exerted a profound influence on the later, highly celebrated temples of Bhubaneswar. Its innovative use of pierced stone lattice windows became a staple of the regional style. Panigrahi asserts this lineage, stating that "The models for the latticed windows in the jagamohana of the Muktesvara temple were in all probability borrowed from Parasuramesvara"1. Thus, the temple serves as the essential bridge between the imported pan-Indian Gupta aesthetic and the indigenous, mature Kalinga style.

## **13\. The Inscriptional Record**

Beyond stylistic and structural analysis, the epigraphic record carved directly into the temple's stonework provides irreplaceable hard data for historical reconstruction. The inscriptions are vital for establishing donor identity, sectarian affiliation, and relative chronology.  
These vital texts were formally brought to the academic community through rigorous epigraphic translation and publication. The definitive scholarly record for these lithic texts was published by A. Ghosh in the seminal journal *Epigraphia Indica*. The publication is formally titled "PARASURAMESVARA TEMPLE INSCRIPTIONS. By A. Ghosh, M. A., Patna," appearing in Volume XXVI of the journal7. As noted previously, these inscriptions are the primary source identifying the Pasupata *acharya* Pramada, proving the temple was an active center of ascetic literacy and patronage1.

## **14\. Conservation History and Structural Modifications**

The Parasuramesvara temple as it stands today is the product of both ancient structural interventions and modern archaeological conservation efforts. The building has undergone significant modifications to ensure its survival.  
As discussed in the structural engineering section, the earliest "conservation" was performed in antiquity when the failing cantilever roof of the *jagamohana* forced builders to fill the open pillar gaps with solid ashlar masonry2. In the modern era, formal archaeological conservation stabilized the monument. R.P. Mohapatra records that the structure was "...restored in 1903 and wherever necessary repaired with old and new materials"6. This 1903 intervention by archaeological authorities ensured the structural integrity of the complex, though the specific details of what was replaced at the sanctum doorway during this campaign are not delineated in the available literature.

## **16\. Epistemological Conflicts in the Source Material**

The scholarly study of the Parasuramesvara temple is characterized by distinct disagreements among experts, primarily regarding its date of origin and physical dimensions.  
**Conflict 1: The Date of Construction**

* **View A (Stylistic/Early):** Scholars focusing on comparative art history, such as R.D. Banerji (in his earlier evaluations addressed by Panigrahi), argue the temple is close to the Gupta era. Quote: "If the Chaitya windows on the Parasuramesvara are in 'the pure early Gupta style,' the date of the temple should not be considered far removed from the Gupta Age." (Panigrahi, *Archaeological Remains at Bhubaneswar*).  
* **View B (Palaeographical/Relative):** Panigrahi argues for a relative date placing it definitively after the Satrughnesvara group based on script evolution. Quote: "Palaeographical features show that the letter forms for ja, ha, and la in the Parasuramesvara inscriptions are more developed than those on the Satrughnesvara, indicating that Satrughnesvara is earlier... than Parasuramesvara." (Panigrahi, *Archaeological Remains at Bhubaneswar*).  
* **View C (Epigraphic/Late):** Later historians relying on the Navagraha-slab push the date into the 8th century. Quote: "It is described as the oldest temple in Orissa, dating no earlier than the 8th century A.D. according to the inscription on its Navagraha-slab." (R. D. Banerji, *History of Orissa Vol II*).

**Conflict 2: Measurements and Dimensions**

* **View A (Height):** Mano Mohan Ganguly measures the towering verticality. Quote: "Mano Mohan Ganguly the height of the Vimana is 60 ft." (R. D. Banerji, *History of Orissa Vol II*).  
* **View B (Depth/Aisles):** Percy Brown provides an alternative dimension focused on internal spatial depth. Quote: "...feet across and 44 feet deep having three aisles of three pillars" (Percy Brown, *Indian Architecture*).  
  *(Note: An alleged IGNCA measurement of 12.80 m is not present in the accepted texts).*
