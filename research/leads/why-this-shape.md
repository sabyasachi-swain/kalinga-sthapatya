# **Explanations for the Design of Odisha Temples**

The architectural design of Odisha (Kalinga) temples has been documented extensively through epigraphical evidence, ASI reports, and scholarly interpretations of primary texts such as the *Silpa Prakasa*. Understanding the structural and conceptual framework of these monuments requires an examination of their foundational texts, the engineering practices employed, and the cosmological symbolism embedded in their construction. The following analysis synthesizes accepted academic and archaeological sources to address the specific design elements of the Orissan temple.

### **The Cosmic Mountain and the Curvilinear Sikhara**

Scholars emphasize that the vertical elevation of the Hindu temple is conceptually tied to sacred geography. Stella Kramrisch explains the nomenclature of the main tower, noting the "form Sikhara, of wdiich the meaning is 'mountain peak' designates particularlv the ... of the Mountain , Meru, Mandara, Kailasa , another type among the twenty temples"1. The form itself is categorized by its shape, with Kramrisch observing that "The curvilinear Sikhara, tv pe II, is the most piohfie , it is built over" the sanctuary1. Furthermore, the temple is recognized as a terrestrial manifestation of cosmic elevations, recorded by scholars as being "no more than an architectural replica of the imagined world mountain Meru"2.

### **The Inner Sanctum: Garbhagriha**

The primary chamber housing the deity is uniformly identified in scholarly literature as the most vital spatial unit of the temple. S. R. Balasubrahmanyam defines its physical placement and significance, stating, "Garbhagriha The garbhagriha is the innermost, and the most sacred, part of" the temple3. Conceptually, the spatial experience of entering this chamber is likened to entering the earth itself. Kramrisch describes the physical environment as being "like a mountain cave or the cavity in the heart. The main..."4. The psychological and ritual purpose of this space is further articulated by Madhu Khanna, who notes, "The Garbhagriha represents the culmination of the individual's search"5.

### **The Temple as a Corporate Body**

Orissan architectural texts meticulously classify the vertical segments of the temple, often assigning terms that correspond to the human body. Nirmal Kumar Bose, in his analysis of the *Canons of Orissan Architecture*, documents the specific vertical specifications of the Rekha temple, listing "the pābhāga, 102-104; the set-back of the pābhaga from. the pişța, 104 ; the fala-jängha, 104-106; its set-back, 106; the Bāndhanā, 106-107; the uparjāngha, 107; the baranda, 108: the gandi, 109-111; its curvature, 111-114... the mastaka, 115-116"6. These terms are explicitly anthropomorphic. The *Silpa Prakasa*, a late medieval text of Odisha translated by Alice Boner and S.R. Rath Sarma, defines these architectural divisions directly as body parts, noting, "Jangha : thigh; the wall-portion of temple building"7.  
The rationale for this anthropomorphic mapping is rooted in the philosophy that the temple is the physical embodiment of the divine. Kramrisch articulates this relationship: "For the living, breathing human bodv is the place where moksa is realized. It is thus that the body. is represented in Indian art... and its scale taken as a module in Indian architecture"4. She concludes that while the statue serves as the manifestation of the deity, "the building is its body and house"4.

### **Structural Mechanics: Dry Masonry and Iron Fastenings**

The construction of these monumental stone structures relied heavily on advanced dry masonry techniques rather than wet mortar. Percy Brown describes the general principle of this engineering, stating, "but there was no mortar, all the masonry being of the dry order. An experiment in jointing stonework, however, was made in the construction of the Deogarh temple, as here the blocks were held together with iron dowels"8.  
In Odisha specifically, the use of iron was prolific and highly developed. K. C. Panigrahi, referencing M. M. Ganguly's *Orissa and Her Remains*, notes that "iron clamps were evi- dently used for the interlocHng of the sculptured ... Orissa and Her Remains^ with the present structure"9. Ganguly's own documentation of conservation efforts mentions "taking out the iron cramps and dowels. 'Latterly, some of the finest"10. P. Neogi's metallurgical survey highlights the sheer scale of this engineering practice in Odisha, recording the presence "of the numerous iron beams. Page 11\. VI. INTRODUCTION. at Puri and Bhubaneswar where as many as 239 pieces have been. counted in the Puri Gunduchibari temple alone"11.

### **The Chariot Motif of Konark**

The Sun Temple at Konark represents a specific typological departure in its external form, designed to mirror the iconography of its presiding deity. Debala Mitra states, "The Sun Temple of Konarak, a monument of vivid architectural imagination, was conceived in the form of a mammoth, twelve-wheeled chariot drawn by seven galloping horses. Dedicated to Surya, the Sun-god, this temple on the coast of Orissa was built in the thirteenth century"10.  
The mythological basis for this design is explicitly linked to the deity's celestial journey. Mitra explains that the temple mimics the "chariot of Surya, the. Sun-god. In Hindu mythology,. Surya is believed to course across the sky in a chariot of seven horses. The twelve pairs of wheels"10. Despite its colossal scale, the structure standing today is incomplete. Mitra clarifies the architectural reality of the site: "Although the tower of the sanctuary collapsed long ago, the Konarak Sun Temple is still a monument of colossal magnificence. What remains of the Sun Temple is the porch or jagamohana, which originally shared a platform with the sanctuary, and the pillared bhoga-mandapa in front"10.

## **B) Conflicts**

No direct textual disagreements between the accepted scholarly sources were found in the provided snippets regarding the structural design, naming conventions, or masonry practices of Orissan temples. While traditional folklore often asserts that no metal was used in these temples, the accepted ASI and archaeological texts consistently agree on the heavy use of dry masonry secured by iron cramps, clamps, and dowels. Similarly, while traditional narratives attribute fixed, rounded construction times and massive craftsman numbers to Konark, the scholarly consensus documented in the snippets focuses purely on the established architectural and epigraphical facts (e.g., construction by Narasimhadeva in the thirteenth century) without validating the folkloric numbers.
