# **Sculptural Themes of Kalinga Temple Architecture: An Evidentiary Report**

## **Analytical Synthesis of Sculptural Motifs**

The temple architecture of Odisha, historically referred to as Kalinga, features a rigorously codified integration of structural engineering and sculptural ornamentation. The fundamental spatial organization of these monuments provides specific architectural canvases—such as architraves, wall niches, and pilasters—upon which specific deities, guardians, and mythical beasts are systematically deployed. For educational observation, particularly in identifying themes visually accessible to younger audiences, several distinct sculptural categories emerge across the monuments of Bhubaneswar and Konark.  
The primary access points of Kalingan temples are frequently guarded by architraves bearing planet-god panels. The evolution of these panels serves as a critical chronological marker for historians and epigraphists. Early temples typically feature an *astagraha* (eight planets) configuration carved onto the lintel above the doorway1. In contrast, later architectural examples transition to a *navagraha* (nine planets) system2.  
The exterior walls of the *vimana* (sanctum) are anchored by the *parshvadevatas* (side deities), which are prominently housed in the central *raha* niches. In Shaiva temples, this familial divine hierarchy is strictly maintained, with Ganesha, Kartikeya, and Parvati serving as the primary *parshvadevatas* occupying these structural focal points3. The broader cosmic and spatial boundaries of the temples are guarded by the *dikpalas* (guardians of the directions) and the *Saptamatrikas* (Seven Mothers), who are frequently depicted in seated postures alongside deities such as Virabhadra and Ganesha5.  
The recessed spaces between the projecting wall segments (*pagas*), known as *anuraha*, are populated by a variety of semi-divine figures and composite beasts that serve both structural and apotropaic functions. Notable among these is the *gaja-simha* (a lion overpowering an elephant). At the structural corners of the recessed *jangha* (wall), craftsmen employed the *dopichha* variation—a lion with a single head and two diverging bodies engineered to wrap around the perpendicular planes5. The facades are further animated by engaged serpent pillars featuring *naga* and *nagi* figures, which are sometimes depicted singularly or intertwined in an embrace (*naga bandha*)5. The apexes of niches and arches are frequently crowned by the *kirtimukha* (Face of Glory), an elaborate mask-like motif7. Lower structural elements and perforated stone windows (grills) feature vibrant depictions of civic and martial life, including animal friezes and intricate carvings of dancing figures, as preserved at the Parasuramesvara and Kapilesvara temples7.  
The chronological culmination of this architectural tradition is the Sun Temple at Konark, constructed in the thirteenth century. Breaking from standard regional templates, the monument was conceptualized entirely as a colossal, twelve-wheeled chariot drawn by seven galloping horses8. It is critical to note that the towering main sanctum of Konark gradually disintegrated, with its final fragment collapsing during a gale in October 18485. The massive structure that remains standing amidst the stone wheels today is the *jagamohana* (frontal porch), which was filled with sand in 1903 to prevent further structural failure5.

## **B) Conflicts**

* **Date of the Parasuramesvara Temple:** There is a chronological disagreement regarding the exact founding century of the Parasuramesvara temple. One scholarly interpretation places it in the seventh century: *"ParaSuramesvara temple at Bhubaneswar belonging to about the seventh century"* (Ajay Mitra Shastri, *Varahamihira and His Times*, snippet 156\)9. Conversely, another analysis suggests a later dating framework based on epigraphy: *"eighth century a.d. probably contains the earliest datable inscriptional"* (Snippet 144\)9.
