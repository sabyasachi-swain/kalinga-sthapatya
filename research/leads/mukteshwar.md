# **Architectural and Historical Analysis of the Mukteshvara Temple, Bhubaneswar**

## **The Sacred Landscape of Ekamrakshetra**

The evolution of Hindu temple architecture in the Indian subcontinent finds one of its most articulate expressions in the coastal region of Odisha, historically referred to as Kalinga. The city of Bhubaneswar, known in ancient times as Ekamrakshetra, served as the epicenter for this architectural efflorescence. The overwhelming sanctity of Ekamrakshetra compelled successive generations of patrons to invest heavily in religious infrastructure, as "the rulers and the ruled, actuated by the hope of an eternal abode in heaven, to vie with one another in embellishing the sacred place"1. This continuous patronage transformed the landscape into a sprawling canvas of structural experimentation, chronicling the transition from the formative shrines of the early medieval period to the monumental edifices of the eleventh and thirteenth centuries.  
At the geographical and chronological heart of this continuum stands the Mukteshvara Temple. Located in the Khordha district of modern Bhubaneswar, the precise coordinates of the temple complex are 20° 14′ 29.7″ N, 85° 51′ 06.5″ E2. Dedicated to the Hindu deity Shiva, the site represents a critical juncture in the maturation of Kalinga architecture4. By analyzing its structural morphology and iconographic program, a profound shift in regional artistic sensibilities becomes evident, marking the formal codification of the Odishan temple vocabulary.

## **Chronological Placement and Epigraphical Ambiguity**

Determining the precise foundation date and the specific royal patron of the Mukteshvara Temple requires navigating a complex historiographical landscape characterized by a lack of direct epigraphical evidence. Extensive archaeological surveys and epigraphic compilations indicate that there is "no inscription definitely connecting a temple of Bhubaneswar with the" exact moment of foundation6. Consequently, scholars have been forced to rely on stylistic seriation and comparative iconography to date the structure.  
There is a broad scholarly consensus placing the construction of the temple in the tenth century CE. Architectural historian Percy Brown assigned a specific chronological marker, estimating its completion at approximately "975 at Bhubaneswar"7. Similarly, broader historical surveys confirm its placement as "Muktesvara (10th century)"8. This timeframe aligns with the ascendancy of the Somavamsi dynasty, also known in regional chronicles as the Keshari kings. Historical analyses demonstrate that the "Somavamsis ruled in Orissa from a.d. 736 to e. a.d. 931 and from e. a.d. 931 to e. A.D. 1110 respectively"9. Furthermore, broader cultural histories note that "It was at the time of the rule of the Kesaris that a large number of Shaiva temples, including the famous Lingaraj temple, were built in and around Bhubaneswar"10. The absence of a dedicated foundation slab or copper-plate grant directly affixed to the temple walls forces historians to categorize the temple's association with a specific Somavamsi ruler as an indirect deduction rather than an established epigraphical fact.

## **Structural Morphology and Typological Evolution**

The architectural classification of Odishan temples traditionally recognizes three primary forms: "rekha, pidha and khakara"11. The Mukteshvara Temple is celebrated for successfully integrating the first two forms into a cohesive unit. The structural layout "consists of two parts namely Jagmohan (assembly area) and Biman, housing the inner sanctum"4. The *biman* (or *deul*) is designed as a *rekha deul*, which is "crowned with a towering spire," while the adjoining *jagamohana* represents an early, stabilized example of the *pidha* order, being "crowned with a stepped pyramidical structure"4.  
The transition observed at Mukteshvara is not merely additive but profoundly structural. Earlier temples in Bhubaneswar often featured flat-roofed rectangular halls. At Mukteshvara, the transition to the mature phase is visible, as it is the site "where the jagamohc^na has almost assumed its regular form"12. Furthermore, the ground plan of the assembly hall exhibits the "pancharatha plan of the jagamohana," indicating a five-fold vertical projection that creates a highly articulated exterior surface11.  
The vertical elevation of the temple's walls, known as the *bada*, demonstrates a parallel leap in complexity. In mature Odishan architecture, the vertical axis consists of the "bada (perpendicular wall), gandi (trunk, the curvilinear spire in the case"1. At Mukteshvara, the *bada* achieves a classical five-part division. The exterior wall "has five divisions \- the lowest being the pabhaga, followed by lower jangha, bandhana, upper jangha and"13. This quintipartite division allowed sculptors an expanded, organized canvas for their decorative programs, moving away from the simpler tripartite divisions of earlier centuries.  
Despite its immense architectural significance, the physical footprint of the temple is remarkably diminutive. Antiquarian R.L. Mitra meticulously documented the structure's proportions, revealing that "The temple is barely 35 feet high and the Jagamohana only 25 feet and the sanctum only 7 feet 7 inches square on the inside"14. Additional measurements from early surveys record various internal chambers with dimensions of "nine feet 5 inches by 6 feet" and a "uniform height of 5 feet 8"9. It is precisely this intimate scale, juxtaposed with flawless execution, that prompted the 19th-century art historian James Fergusson to declare it "The gem of Orissan Architecture"15. This moniker underscores the analytical perspective that architectural value in the Kalinga tradition is not strictly a function of monumental scale, but of harmonious proportion and sculptural density.

## **Iconographic Syntax and Artistic Synthesis**

The decorative program of the Mukteshvara Temple is characterized by an unprecedented delicacy and an intriguing thematic breadth. The most iconic structural element associated with the site is its freestanding arched gateway, or *torana*. This "Torana, Mukteivara Temple" features a heavily carved archway9. The detailing of this gateway includes a prominent "Kirtti-mukha with an elaborate design below"9, a motif that serves both an apotropaic function (warding off evil) and a decorative one. The presence of a *torana*—a feature more commonly associated with early Buddhist stupa enclosures (such as Sanchi)—suggests the deliberate retention and adaptation of ancient spatial demarcations within medieval Hindu temple complexes.  
The exterior walls of the temple host a vast array of iconographic representations that point to a period of intense religious syncretism in tenth-century Odisha. While the primary deity is Shiva—evidenced by panels such as the "Gajasura-SanihSra-Murtti" (Shiva slaying the elephant demon) and the depiction of the Shaivite ascetic "Lakulria" (Lakulisa)9—the temple also features figures from competing religious traditions. Archaeologist Krishna Chandra Panigrahi documented the unmistakable presence of a "DhySni Buddha" and a "Jaina Tirthahkara" carved directly into the temple's stonework9. The inclusion of Buddhist and Jain icons on a royal Shaiva monument suggests that the patron dynasty actively sought to project an image of universal theological accommodation, visually unifying the diverse socio-religious factions residing within their domain.  
Further demonstrating a departure from rigid regional orthodoxies, the interior of the *jagamohana* contains a highly unusual feature for Odishan architecture: a sculpted ceiling. While most contemporary temples in the region left the interior corbelled roofs unadorned, Mukteshvara features a "lotus placed upside down decorated on the ceiling of the Mukhasala"16. The "tradition of carving the ceiling with a lotus design goes back to the Gupta age," suggesting that the artisans at Mukteshvara were consciously importing or reviving classical pan-Indian motifs to elevate the aesthetic prestige of the structure11.  
The exterior surfaces are pierced by a beautifully executed "perforated wndow" (stone lattice), which allows filtered light into the interior spaces17. The walls are further populated by dynamic secular and semi-divine figures, including "a female warrior" and a serpentine "nagi"17. Intriguingly, the sculptors also embedded a unique visual puzzle into the stonework; as Panigrahi notes, "In the temple of Muktesvara there is a decorative device of enig- matic and amusing character that appears in two" instances9. Such details indicate that the artisans were afforded a degree of creative autonomy, weaving playful, illusionistic elements into the highly regulated, canonical framework of the temple's surface.  
From a stylistic perspective, the sculptural forms exhibit traits that connect Kalinga art to broader subcontinental currents. For instance, scholars have noted compositional parallels where the "Muktesvara and the Badami Nataraja occurs in the Cave no. I at Badami," highlighting a cross-pollination of artistic ideas between the Chalukyan artisans of the Deccan and the Kalinga craftsmen18. Furthermore, the prevalence of "Sun images represented in the temple sculptures of... Muktesvara (10th century)" underscores the deep-rooted, widespread influence of solar worship in Odisha, which would eventually culminate in the massive Sun Temple at Konark centuries later11.

## **Historiographical Conflicts**

The most notable historiographical conflict regarding the Mukteshvara Temple concerns the exact dating of its construction. Because there is "no inscription definitely connecting a temple of Bhubaneswar with the" foundation6, scholars have derived varying degrees of chronological precision based on stylistic analysis. Percy Brown posits a highly specific date, arguing for "975 at Bhubaneswar"7. In contrast, Krishna Chandra Panigrahi opts for a more cautious methodological approach, stating: "Our main attempt here has been to assign the numerous monuments of Bhubaneswar to the broad political epochs, but not to fix their accurate dates, which in many cases is precluded by the nature of the data available"9. Consequently, general historical texts, such as those by K. S. Behera, default to placing the monument broadly in the "10th century" without committing to a specific decade8. This divergence is less a contradiction of facts than a reflection of differing scholarly tolerances for estimating dates in the absence of primary epigraphical evidence.

## **Gaps in the Archaeological Record**

Despite exhaustive consultation of the available academic literature, archaeological survey reports, and edited primary texts, several specific data points regarding the Mukteshvara Temple remain absent from the accessible record.  
Most critically, the name of the exact royal patron or architect responsible for commissioning the temple is not recorded in the available texts; while the era belongs to the Keshari/Somavamsi dynasty, the specific ruler remains unverified. Furthermore, the texts do not clarify whether active, daily ritual worship continues at the site today (classifying it as a living temple).  
Regarding architectural mechanics and materials, the sources lack explicit mention of the specific stone types used for each component of the building, the location of the quarry sources, and the precise engineering methods utilized by the builders, such as the use of earthen ramps, corbelling techniques, or the application of iron cramps and dowels for dry masonry stabilization. The exact orientation of the main entrance (the cardinal direction it faces) is similarly undocumented in the provided material.  
While the height of the main tower is established at 35 feet, comprehensive dimensional data—such as the exact measurements of the external platform, the boundary enclosure, or the dimensions of an adjacent tank—are missing. The modern conservation history of the site, including detailed records of historical damage, collapses, or subsequent structural interventions and repairs by the Public Works Department (PWD) or the Archaeological Survey of India (ASI), is also absent from the consulted documentation. Finally, there is no textual evidence providing a definitive relative date for the construction of the *torana* (arched gateway) compared to the main sanctuary, leaving it unclear if they were executed simultaneously or in successive phases. These gaps highlight areas where the historical record remains silent, requiring future archaeological investigation to construct a fully comprehensive profile of the site.
