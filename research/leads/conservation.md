## B) Conflicts

1. **Reason for the Collapse of Konark's Sanctuary (*Deul*)**:
   - *Percy Brown's View (Incompletion Theory)*: Percy Brown (*Indian Architecture [Buddhist and Hindu Periods]*, 1959, p. 128) asserted that the soaring curvilinear tower (*rekha deul*) collapsed because it was never structurally completed, arguing that the builders overreached their technical engineering capacity on marshy foundations before the crowning finial (*kalasa*) could be installed.
   - *Archaeological Consensus (Complete and Consecrated)*: Krishna Chandra Panigrahi (*Archaeological Remains at Bhubaneswar*, 1961), Debala Mitra (*Konarak*, 1968/2003, pp. 11–13), and Karuna Sagar Behera (*Konark: The Black Pagoda*, 1996) decisively refute Brown's hypothesis. They cite copper-plate charters of the Ganga dynasty (e.g., the Kendupatna copper plates of King Narasimhadeva II, dated Saka 1217 / 1295 CE) that record Narasimhadeva I as having built and consecrated the *mahat-kutira* (great cottage/sanctuary) of the Sun God; epigraphical references to the temple being in active worship; and Abul-Fazl’s 16th-century *Ain-i-Akbari*, which recorded the complete, awe-inspiring edifice still standing. Mitra concludes the collapse was gradual, initiated by desecration and structural decay.

2. **1903 PWD Restoration Methodology vs. Modern Conservation Philosophy**:
   - *M. H. Arnott / Colonial PWD Practice (1898–1903)*: Arnott's primary objective was pragmatic structural stability to prevent collapse. Where ancient monolithic elements were fractured, the PWD completely removed them and substituted modern assembled masonry pillars (as in the Parasuramesvara *jagamohana*), replaced intricately carved original sills and lintels with plain dressed stone blocks, and erected *dopikha-simhas* (double-bodied lions) on the mastaka to weigh down the superstructure.
   - *Critical Modern Scholarly Appraisal*: Thomas E. Donaldson (*Hindu Temple Art of Orissa*, Vol. 1, 1985, p. 30) and later architectural historians note that while the PWD unquestionably rescued monuments like Parasuramesvara from imminent collapse, their invasive methods introduced non-original architectural members and permanently altered the aesthetic symmetry and historical authenticity of 7th-century Kalinga architecture.

3. **Efficacy of the 1901–1903 Jagamohana Sand-Filling**:
   - *1901–1903 PWD / Bishan Swarup Perspective*: Regarded as an essential, life-saving engineering intervention. With the ceiling stones dislodging and the corbelled horizontal courses threatening to implode under their deadweight, packing the internal chamber completely with dry sand was considered the only viable means to counteract internal thrust and support the pyramid.
   - *Post-1950s Archaeological and Scientific Perspective*: Debala Mitra's 1950s drilling tests and CBRI's 2013–2018 non-destructive radar and endoscopic investigations established that the sealed sand environment acted as a moisture trap, drawing in rainwater and creating a microclimate of high relative humidity that fostered biogrowth, decomposed the internal khondalite blocks, and left an unsupported 17-foot void as the sand compacted, thereby necessitating its controlled evacuation.

4. **Architectural Terminology for Base Mouldings (*Pabhaga*)**:
   - *Mano Mohan Ganguly (1912)*: Uses the terms *Pada*, *Kumuda*, and *Basanta* to describe the three basal mouldings in early temples (*Orissa and Her Remains*, 1912).
   - *N. K. Bose (1932) and Panigrahi (1961)*: Canonical textual records (*Silpa Sastras*) and later scholarly consensus standardize the three mouldings as *khura* (hoof), *kumbha* (pot), and *pata* / *patta* (band).
