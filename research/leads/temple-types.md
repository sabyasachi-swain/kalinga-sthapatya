# **The Architectural Typology of Kalinga Temples: An Analysis of Rekha, Pidha, and Khakhara Orders**

## **The Canonical Basis of Classification**

The categorization of the structural forms within the temple architecture of Odisha (historically Kalinga) does not originate from modern academic conventions but is firmly rooted in indigenous canonical literature. The exact terminology utilized to differentiate the architectural forms is derived from the local Silpa Sastras. As established by the textual evidence, "The local Slipa Sastra texts mention three types of structures, rekha, pidha and khakara"1.  
Modern comprehension of these ancient treatises was significantly advanced by scholars such as Nirmal Kumar Bose, who translated and analyzed primary texts including the *Bhuvanapradipa* in his 1932 work *Canons of Orissan Architecture*. Bose's examination of the texts reveals a classification system that identifies three primary indigenous styles and one external style. The canonical texts specify, "Four different types-Rekha, Bhadra, Khakhara, Gouriya, the last an exotic, 78; analysis of the three prevalent types. in their component parts, piṣṭaganḍi, mastaka, 78-79"2. Furthermore, the ancient texts differentiate these structures based on specific architectural segments, particularly the superstructure. The treatises denote "gandi a distinguishing factor, in the Rekha, 79, Bhadra. 79-80, and Khakharā temples 80-81,;"2. This confirms that the three-type classification—*rekha*, *pidha* (or *bhadra*), and *khakhara*—is an established, canonical typology prescribed by the original artisans and texts, rather than a retroactive scholarly construct.

## **The Rekha Deul: Curvilinear Morphology and Sanctum Architecture**

The *rekha deul* is the most prominent structural form in the Orissan architectural tradition, functioning primarily as the main sanctuary or *garbhagriha*. The defining characteristic of the *rekha* order lies in the specific geometry of its superstructure, known canonically as the *gandi*. Architectural surveys and textual translations confirm that the shape is defined by its vertical, curved elevation. The literature states, "A rekha deul is characterised by a curvilinear tower"3. The term itself is inextricably linked to this form, as "The Orissan sikJiara tvpe temple is called rekha deul etymologically, 'curvilinear"4.  
Functionally, the *rekha deul* serves as the primary repository for the cult image. According to structural analyses, "The central shrine with its curvilinear spire is known in Orissan terminology as Rekha Deul"5. While it houses deities of various sectarian affiliations (primarily Saiva and Vaishnava), it is structurally designed to act as the cosmic axis of the temple complex.  
A critical, albeit debated, early example of the *rekha* order is the Parasuramesvara temple in Bhubaneswar. Scholarly consensus on its exact dating is fractured, reflecting the complexities of early epigraphic and stylistic interpretation. Certain archaeological assessments document the structure as "Parasuramesvara temple at Bhubaneswar, dated to seventh century AD"6. Conversely, other epigraphical analyses place it slightly later, describing it as "ParaSuramesvara temple at Bhubaneswar belonging to about the seventh ... eighth century a.d."7, while some specific historical surveys state definitively that it belongs to the "eighth century A.D.®"8. Despite this chronological variance, the Parasuramesvara temple remains a foundational example of the *rekha* architectural style.

## **The Pidha Deul (Bhadra): Pyramidal Structures and the Assembly Hall**

Operating in direct spatial and structural tandem with the *rekha deul* is the *pidha deul*, which is canonically referred to as the *bhadra* in the Silpa texts. In stark contrast to the curvilinear verticality of the *rekha*, the *pidha* order is defined by its stepped, pyramidal roof. The structural definition provided by historians notes a "pidha temple with pyramidal roof instead of being a rectangular structure"9. The mechanics of this roof are explicitly detailed in architectural studies, which state that it is a "pidha deul temple of which the pyramidal roof is made of pidhas;"3, with the *pidhas* being horizontal platforms or tiers that diminish in size as they ascend to the *mastaka* (crowning element)5.  
The primary function of the *pidha deul* is to serve as the *jagamohana* (assembly hall or porch), providing a congregational space directly preceding the sanctum. The historical development of this structure reveals its evolution from an attached appendage to a monumental independent unit. As recorded in the architectural chronicles, "canons of Orissan architecture it is known as the Pidha or Bhadra\* type which is first found as the porch of the main iikkara or rekha temples and then as an independent unit"11.  
The most majestic surviving example of the *pidha deul* is the Sun Temple at Konarak. Historical documentation establishes its creation under the Eastern Ganga dynasty: "Erected towards the middle of the thirteenth century by the Eastern Ganga king, Narasimhadeva, it is unrivalled for its unique architecture and exquisite sculptures"3. Crucially, the main *rekha* tower (sanctum) of Konarak has not survived, leaving the monumental *pidha* hall as the primary standing structure. Archaeological records confirm, "Although the tower of the sanctuary collapsed long ago, the Konarak Sun Temple is still a monument of colossal magnificence. What remains of the Sun Temple is the porch or jagamohana"3.

## **The Khakhara Deul: Barrel-Vaulted Forms and Sakta Affiliations**

The *khakhara deul* diverges significantly from the *rekha* and *pidha* orders in both its structural geometry and its strict cultic affiliations. The defining characteristic of this order is its elongated, barrel-vaulted roof. Architectural historians trace the morphological lineage of this roof to ancient, perishable construction materials. The documented origin states: "The form of the roof of the khdkhard temples is a modified survival of the primitive halls with a semi-cylindrical roof resting on a framework of timber or bent split bamboos (in the case of a thatched roof) and having a gable or hemispherical end at either side and a ridge along the crest-line"12. The indigenous nomenclature is derived from its visual similarity to local agricultural produce, specifically "due to the faint resemblance of the barrel-vaulted elongated roof of its temples with a variety of pumpkin-gourd, called kakhdru in Oriya"12.  
Furthermore, the *Bhuvanapradipa* text outlines specific sub-varieties of the *khakhara* order. As analyzed by scholars, "the measurements and specifications of three different varieties of this kind of temples are given. These are Dravida, Badabhi 8 and Kosoli"12.  
Unlike the *rekha* and *pidha* types, the *khakhara* temple is inextricably linked to the Sakta cult (the worship of the Goddess). The oblong ground plan of the structure is uniquely suited to house multi-deity panels, such as the Sapta-Matrikas (Seven Mothers). The scholarly consensus is explicit regarding this association: "The khakhra temple, used for Devi worship"1. Furthermore, detailed analysis of extant structures confirms, "The available evidence thus indicates that temples of this Order in Orissa belonged to the Sakta cult, 6 \* though the converse is not true, for Sakti is also seen en¬ shrined in temples of other Orders, e.g. the Rekha temples of Mohini and Chitrakarini at Bhubaneswar"12. This establishes that while *khakhara* temples are universally dedicated to Sakti, Sakti worship is not exclusively confined to the *khakhara* form.  
Prominent examples of the *khakhara* order include the Vaital Deul in Bhubaneswar and the Varahi temple at Chaurasi. The Vaital Deul is a critical early example, with art historical documentation dating its intricate sculptural and architectural components to the "Eighth century"14. The Varahi temple at Chaurasi serves as another primary example, uniquely dedicated to the goddess Varahi12.

## **Architectural Synthesis: Component Parts of a Single Shrine**

The maturity of the Kalinga architectural style is defined by the integration of these distinct orders into a unified sacred complex. While the *rekha*, *pidha*, and *khakhara* possess independent structural logic, the standard Orissan temple relies on the combination of the *rekha* and *pidha* to facilitate ritual worship. The synthesis of the curvilinear sanctuary and the pyramidal assembly hall is the fundamental basis of the regional style. This is explicitly stated in structural analyses: "Rekha deul and Pidhd deul forms two component parts of a single shrine"9. This combination allows the structure to fulfill the dual requirements of providing a highly restricted, vertical cosmic axis for the deity (the *rekha* sanctum) and a voluminous, horizontal space for congregational assembly (the *pidha* hall), thereby creating a complete architectural and spiritual unit.
