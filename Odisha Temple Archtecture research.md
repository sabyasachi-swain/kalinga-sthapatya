# Odisha Temple Architecture (Kalinga Architecture): An Evidence-Based Research Report

## TL;DR
- Kalinga (Odishan) temple architecture is a distinct regional branch of the North Indian Nagara tradition that evolved almost continuously from c. 6th–7th century CE to the 13th century CE, reaching its zenith under the Somavamshi and Eastern Ganga dynasties; it is organized around three temple types (Rekha, Pidha, Khakhara deula) and a body-analogy vertical scheme (bada–gandi–mastaka), and is documented uniquely by the surviving Sanskrit silpa text *Silpa Prakasa*.
- The canonical chronology runs from the Parasuramesvara (c. 650 CE, Shailodbhava) through Vaital Deula (8th c., Bhauma-Kara), Mukteshvara (c. 950–975 CE) and Brahmesvara (dated inscriptionally to 1058/1060 CE, Somavamshi), to Lingaraja (c. 1000–1100 CE), the Jagannath Temple at Puri (rebuilt from c. 1112 CE under Anantavarman Chodaganga) and Konark (c. 1250 CE, Narasimhadeva I), the last great monument before the tradition's decline.
- The definitive modern scholarly reference is T.E. Donaldson, *Hindu Temple Art of Orissa* (3 vols, Brill, 1985–87); the key primary text is *Silpa Prakasa* (Boner & Rath Sarma, Brill 1966; Baumer, Das et al. eds., Motilal Banarsidass 2005); Konark is a UNESCO World Heritage Site (1984, ref. 246) whose famous colonial sand-fill of the Jagamohana is the central ongoing conservation problem.

## Key Findings
1. **A continuous, dateable evolution.** Odishan scholars (K.S. Behera, A.N. Parida, R.P. Mohapatra) divide the tradition into a Formative Phase (7th–9th c.), a Transitional Phase (10th–11th c.), and a Mature Phase (11th–13th c.), with the pre-temple rock-cut Jain caves of Udayagiri–Khandagiri (2nd–1st c. BCE, Kharavela) as the deep antecedent. There is remarkable stylistic continuity across dynastic changes.
2. **A codified typology and vocabulary.** Rekha (curvilinear sanctum tower), Pidha/Bhadra (pyramidal-roofed hall), and Khakhara (barrel-vaulted, Shakta) deulas; the four-part temple (vimana, jagamohana, natamandira, bhogamandapa); vertical divisions bada–gandi–mastaka; the panchanga/saptanga bada systems; and the ratha (paga) classification (triratha to navaratha).
3. **Inscriptionally anchored dates exist for some temples** (Brahmesvara 1058/1060 CE; Ananta Vasudeva 1278 CE), while others (Parasuramesvara, Mukteshvara, Lingaraja, Konark) rest on stylistic dating and disputed attributions.
4. **A unique textual tradition** survives in the *Silpa Prakasa* of Ramachandra Kaulacara, plus the *Bhubaneswar Silpa* tradition and N.K. Bose's *Canons of Orissan Architecture* (1932).
5. **Dry (mortar-less) masonry** with iron cramps/dowels and three stones (khondalite, laterite, chlorite) characterizes construction; Konark's decay is partly attributable to poor-grade khondalite and a coastal saline environment.
6. **Konark's conservation** is dominated by the British-era sand-fill of the Jagamohana (1901–1904) and its subsequent subsidence, tracked through UNESCO/ICOMOS reports since 1997 and an active ASI sand-removal/structural evaluation program.

## Details

### 1. Historical evolution and phases
The deepest antecedent of monumental architecture in the region is the rock-cut tradition of the **Udayagiri and Khandagiri caves** near Bhubaneswar, excavated as residential cells for Jain ascetics under the Chedi (Mahameghavahana) king **Kharavela** in the 2nd–1st century BCE. The **Hathigumpha inscription** in Udayagiri Cave 14 — a 17-line Prakrit record in Brahmi script — is the principal source for Kharavela's reign; the minor Kharavela inscriptions were published by R.D. Banerji in *Epigraphia Indica* Vol. XIII (1915–16). These caves are not temples but establish an early, sophisticated tradition of rock carving, guardian figures, and narrative relief in Kalinga.

Structural temple-building begins in earnest under the **Shailodbhavas** in the 7th century. The Odisha government's own scholarly summary (A. Sahoo, *Odisha Review*, 2012) describes the standard three-phase scheme: **Formative** (early Nagara temples, triratha plan, trianga bada, flat-roofed rectangular mukhasalas), **Transitional**, and **Mature** (pancharatha/saptaratha plans, panchanga bada, pidha jagamohanas, and the addition of natamandira and bhogamandapa). Bhubaneswar — the "Temple City" (ancient Ekamra Kshetra) — today preserves over 700 surviving small and large temples of an estimated 7,000 that once stood there, spanning these phases in the Kalinga (Nagara) style.

The **zenith** (11th–13th c.) coincides with Somavamshi consolidation at Bhubaneswar and the imperial Eastern Ganga period, producing Lingaraja, the Puri Jagannath temple, and finally Konark. After Konark (mid-13th c.), no comparably ambitious temples were built; the tradition weakened, though temple-building in a late Kalingan idiom continued under the Gajapatis into the 15th–16th centuries (e.g., in Mayurbhanj and Baripada).

### 2. Architectural typology and components
Kalingan silpa texts recognize three deula types:
- **Rekha Deula** — a "straight-line" curvilinear sugar-loaf tower over the garbhagriha; divided into pista (optional platform, mainly Ganga-period), bada, gandi, and mastaka/sira. Example: Lingaraja, Jagannath.
- **Pidha (Bhadra) Deula** — a square hall whose gandi is a stepped pyramid of horizontal platforms (pidhas), arranged in tiers (potala); crowned by beki, ghanta (bell), amla, khapuri, kalasa. Used for the jagamohana and later halls (e.g., the surviving Konark Jagamohana).
- **Khakhara Deula** — oblong plan with a barrel-vaulted (wagon-roof) mastaka resembling a *kakharu* (pumpkin); reserved mainly for Shakta/goddess temples (Vaital Deula, Varahi at Chaurasi, Gaurī shrines). The *Odisha Review* notes only about six Khakhara examples in Bhubaneswar, but the form was widely used as a decorative miniature motif.

Two additional forms noted in the literature are the **Gauriya** temple (a late, hybrid type in Mayurbhanj and Puri) and the **hypaethral** (roofless, circular) **Chausath Yogini** temples at Hirapur (near Bhubaneswar) and Ranipur-Jharial.

**Structural components** of a fully developed temple, in ascending ritual order along an east–west axis: **Vimana** (sanctum/deul), **Jagamohana** (assembly/porch hall), **Nata Mandira** (dance hall), and **Bhoga Mandapa** (hall of offerings). A local craftsman's metaphor holds the vimana to be the "bridegroom" and the jagamohana the "bride."

**Vertical divisions** (body analogy — the temple as the Cosmic Being/Purusha):
- **Bada** (lower limb/wall). Early temples have a three-part (trianga) bada — pabhaga, jangha, baranda. Mature temples have a five-part (panchanga) bada — **pabhaga, tala jangha, bandhana, upara jangha, baranda**. The pabhaga itself comprises mouldings (khura, kumbha, pata, kani, basanta). (A saptanga/seven-part bada appears in the most developed monuments.)
- **Gandi** (body/tower). In rekha temples it curves inward convexly, divided into pagas; kanika pagas are subdivided into storeys (bhumi) marked by bhumi-amla discs; often decorated with anga-sikharas and a raha chaitya-window.
- **Mastaka** (head/crown) — beki, amla(ka), khapuri, kalasa, and ayudha (deity's weapon), topped by the dhvaja.

**Plan/ratha classification:** the interior is square, but external offset projections (rathas/pagas) yield **triratha, pancharatha, saptaratha, navaratha** plans. The central projection is the raha paga, flanked by anuratha, anuraha, and kanika pagas.

### 3. Major temples — dating and attribution
- **Parasuramesvara Temple** (Bhubaneswar): the best-preserved early temple, generally dated **c. 650 CE**, Shailodbhava period. K.C. Panigrahi placed it at 650 CE; some historians favor an early 8th-century date after the Satrughnesvara/Bharatesvara/Lakshmanesvara group; Fergusson speculated as early as c. 500 CE. It is the first Odishan temple to add a jagamohana to the vimana, and it carries eight planets (astagraha) on the door lintel — an archaic feature (later temples show nine, the navagraha).
- **Vaital Deula** (Bhubaneswar): the type-site Khakhara temple, dedicated to **Chamunda**, c. **8th century**, Bhauma-Kara period; tradition ascribes it to Queen Tribhuvana Mahadevi. Its three spires ("Tini-mundia") are unusual; strong Tantric/Kapalika associations.
- **Mukteshvara Temple** (Bhubaneswar): c. **950–975 CE**, the earliest work of the Somavamshi period and a "miniature gem," famous for its free-standing **torana** (arched gateway). Percy Brown dated it c. 950 CE; K.C. Panigrahi to 966 CE, attributing it to Yayati I (an attribution most scholars do not accept as proven). It marks the culmination of earlier developments and inaugurates a century of experiment.
- **Rajarani Temple** (Bhubaneswar): c. **1000 CE** (disputed). Percy Brown and S.K. Sarasvati placed it in the 11th–12th c.; Panigrahi between Mukteshvara and Lingaraja; Fergusson dated its beginning to 1105 CE; George Michell places it contemporaneous with Lingaraja. Noted for its dikpalas (directional guardians) and an angasikhara-clustered gandi showing affinity with Central Indian (Khajuraho) form. It enshrines no deity today (hence "Rajarani," possibly after its dull-red "rajarania" sandstone).
- **Brahmesvara Temple** (Bhubaneswar): securely dated by a (now-lost, recorded) inscription to the **18th regnal year of Somavamshi king Udyotakesari = 1058 CE** (some sources 1060 CE), built by his mother **Kolavati Devi**. It is the first panchayatana/pancharatana temple in Bhubaneswar and gives the first inscriptional reference to the **devadasi** tradition in the region.
- **Lingaraja Temple** (Bhubaneswar): the great Shaiva temple to Tribhuvanesvara, generally dated **c. 1000–1100 CE**. Some accounts attribute the main temple to Somavamshi Yayati I (1025–1040); the natamandira was added c. 1099–1104 CE; later Ganga inscriptions (e.g., a Saka 1094 = 1172 CE grant by Rajaraja II) record continuing patronage. It represents the mature Odishan style at monumental scale (deul c. 55 m).
- **Jagannath Temple, Puri**: rebuilt in stone by Eastern Ganga king **Anantavarman Chodaganga** (r. 1078–1150); construction of the present great temple began after his conquest of Utkala in 1112 CE, with a 1134–35 CE inscription recording his donation; completed under **Anangabhima Deva III** (r. c. 1211–1238). Attribution rests on the **Kendupatna copper-plate** of a descendant (Narasimhadeva II). The Jagannath (Purushottam) cult was thereby "raised to the status of an Imperial cult."
- **Konark Sun Temple** (Puri district): dedicated to Surya, built c. **1250 CE** by Eastern Ganga king **Narasimhadeva I** (r. 1238–1264). Conceived as a colossal stone chariot with 24 wheels and seven horses. The main **vimana**, estimated at roughly **229 feet (c. 70 m) tall** (James Harle judged the original tower "must have stood to a height of some 225 feet"), has collapsed; the surviving pidha **Jagamohana** stands about **128 feet (c. 39 m)** high. The palm-leaf construction chronicles (*Baya Cakada / Baya Chandrika*), published as Boner, Rath Sarma & Das, *New Light on the Sun Temple of Koṇārka* (1972), place construction across 1246–1258, with consecration reportedly in 1258 CE. UNESCO dates it to Narasimhadeva I's reign (AD 1238–1264).
- **Ananta Vasudeva Temple** (Bhubaneswar): the only major surviving Vaishnava temple in Bhubaneswar, dated by a commemorative inscription (now in the British Museum) to **Saka 1200 = 1278 CE**, built by Queen **Chandrika (Chandra Devi)**, daughter of Anangabhima III, during the reign of Bhanudeva I. In form it echoes Lingaraja at reduced scale; extensively renovated by the Marathas in the late 17th c.

### 4. Construction techniques and materials
Odishan temples were built in **dry (mortar-less) masonry**: sized stones laid horizontally, held by a "system of counterpoise" (balance and equilibrium) and secured with **iron cramps and dowels**; corbelling was used to span/roof interiors. Three stones dominate:
- **Khondalite** (garnetiferous gneiss) — the main body and most sculpture; prone to weathering, a principal cause of Konark's decay.
- **Laterite** — foundations, cores, platforms, staircases, and compound walls.
- **Chlorite** (locally *muguni*, green) — doorframes, simhasanas, cult icons, and fine sculpture; the most weather-resistant.

Around Bhubaneswar the local **Athgarh (Upper Gondwana) sandstone** was extensively used from the 7th century. Because none of these stones outcrops immediately at Konark, material was transported considerable distances, then dressed, hoisted (by rollers/pulleys/ramps), and fitted so precisely that joints are nearly invisible. The organization of labor is described in the silpa literature: a chief patron (**Kartā**, usually the king), a master-architect/**Sutragrahani** (also Mahapatra), and the **Bardhanikas** (masons) who precut stones per the **bhunaksa** (ground plan).

### 5. Primary textual sources
- **Silpa Prakasa** by **Ramachandra Kaulacara** (Ramachandra Mahapatra Kaula Bhattaraka) — a medieval Orissan Sanskrit temple-architecture text, most likely 9th–10th century CE (Boner favored a later date nearer Konark; the tradition it draws on, the *Saudhikagama*, must predate the 10th c.). Discovered on palm-leaf and translated/annotated by **Alice Boner and Sadasiva Rath Sarma**, published **Leiden: E.J. Brill, 1966** (lvii, 166 pp. + 102 pp. Sanskrit text, 72 plates). It uniquely describes a Tantric temple type, **Vimanamalini** (sub-types Padmagarbha and Kamagarbha — the latter possibly the 10th-c. Varahi temple at Chaurasi), and — uniquely in all silpa literature — the **yantras** (symbolic diagrams) underlying both architecture and sculpture. A later edition was prepared by **Bettina Baumer, Rajendra Prasad Das (and Sadananda Das)** with the Boner/Rath Sarma translation, published by IGNCA / **Motilal Banarsidass (2005)**.
- **N.K. Bose, *Canons of Orissan Architecture*** (R. Chatterjee, Calcutta, 1932) — passages from silpa manuscripts (Sanskrit/Oriya) with English commentary.
- Other Odishan silpa/agama texts referenced in the scholarship: the **Saudhikagama** (the encyclopedic source cited by the Silpa Prakasa), the **Bhubanapradipa**, **Silpasarini**, and the *Svarnnadri Mahodaya* (a medieval mahatmya text mentioning Tantric practice west of Bindusagar, matching Vaital Deula).

### 6. Iconography and sculpture
Kalingan temples carry a dense, prescribed sculptural program. **Parsva-devatas** (side/niche deities) occupy the raha niches of the bada (e.g., Kartikeya, Ganesha, Parvati/Gaja-Lakshmi on a Shaiva temple; Varaha, Trivikrama, Narasimha on a Vaishnava one). **Dikpalas** (eight directional guardians) and the **navagraha** (nine planets, on the door lintel of later temples) are standard. Female figures — **alasa kanyas** ("indolent maidens," a type of surasundari), collectively **nari-bandha / alasa-bandha** — are a signature motif; the *Silpa Prakasa* enumerates **sixteen types** (Torana, Mugdha, Manini, Dalamalika, Padmagandha, Darpana, Vinyasa, Ketakibharana, Matrmurti, Chamara, Gunthana, Nartaki, Sukasarika, Nupurapadika, Mardala, and others) and declares (I.392–393) that "as a house without a wife… so without the figure of women, art will be deficient and bear no fruit." Also prescribed: **naga-nagis** (serpent deities), **mithuna/keli** (amorous couples) and erotic scenes, **gaja-vidala/vyala** motifs, and the symbolic **vajramastaka** on the sikhara front. The erotic and female imagery is widely interpreted (following Boner) as expressing Tantric fertility symbolism, with yantras concealed beneath. The *Silpa Prakasa* is the principal textual authority prescribing where each figure is placed on the vimana and mukhasala.

### 7. Inscriptions and epigraphy
Key epigraphic anchors: the **Hathigumpha inscription** (Kharavela; *Epigraphia Indica* XIII); the **Brahmesvara inscription** (Udyotakesari, 1058 CE — the firmest foundation date in Bhubaneswar, though the stone was removed to Calcutta/Kolkata); the **Ananta Vasudeva inscription** (Saka 1200 / 1278 CE, now in the British Museum, naming Queen Chandrika); the **Kendupatna copper-plate grant** (of Narasimhadeva II, used to attribute the Puri Jagannath temple's rebuilding to Chodaganga and to trace Ganga genealogy); Chodaganga's 1134–35 donative inscription at Puri; and Lingaraja grants of Rajaraja II (Saka 1094 / 1172 CE). Numerous Bhauma-Kara copper-plates (e.g., the Chaurasi plate of Sivakara-deva II, 809–836 CE) fix the dynasty's chronology. **Caveat:** contrary to some popular summaries, the Kendupatna plate is essentially genealogical (of Narasimhadeva II) and does not itself directly date the construction of Konark; Konark's date rests on the palm-leaf chronicles, stylistic analysis, and the reign-dates of Narasimhadeva I (whose 1246 Kapilash inscription first uses the title Gajapati).

### 8. Religious context
Odisha uniquely preserves all major sectarian traditions in stone. **Shaiva** temples dominate Bhubaneswar (Ekamra Kshetra), from Parasuramesvara to Lingaraja. **Shakta/Tantric** worship is expressed in the Khakhara temples (Vaital, Varahi) and the hypaethral **Chausath Yogini** shrines (Hirapur, Ranipur-Jharial). **Vaishnava** monuments include Ananta Vasudeva and, supremely, the **Jagannath cult** at Puri — a syncretic tradition (Jagannath/Purushottam absorbing tribal, Shaiva, Shakta, Buddhist and Vaishnava strands) that became the state cult under the Gangas and Gajapatis, with Anangabhima III dedicating the kingdom to Jagannath as the deity's "Rauta" (deputy). **Saura (solar)** worship culminates at Konark (and the Arasavalli sun temple in Srikakulam). **Buddhist** and **Jain** traditions are strong regional presences — the Udayagiri–Khandagiri Jain caves, and the Buddhist "Diamond Triangle" of Ratnagiri–Udayagiri–Lalitgiri, with Buddhist finds also at Khiching.

### 9. Dynasties and patronage
- **Shailodbhavas** (7th–8th c.): earliest structural temples (Parasuramesvara, Satrughnesvara group) — Formative phase.
- **Bhauma-Karas** (8th–10th c.): Vaital Deula and the Khakhara/Shakta-Tantric tradition; notable female rulers (Tribhuvana Mahadevi).
- **Somavamshis** (Kesaris, 10th–11th c.): Mukteshvara, Rajarani, Brahmesvara, Lingaraja — the Transitional-to-Mature transformation; shifting capital toward Bhubaneswar.
- **Eastern Gangas** (11th–15th c.): the imperial climax — Jagannath at Puri (Chodaganga), Ananta Vasudeva (Chandrika), and Konark (Narasimhadeva I). Their early capital was **Kalinganagara = Mukhalingam** (Srikakulam), moved to Kataka (Cuttack) by Chodaganga in 1112/1122.
- **Gajapatis** (Suryavamshi, 15th–16th c.): institutionalized the Jagannath state cult and continued late-Kalingan temple building.

### 10. Regional variations
Beyond Bhubaneswar: **Jajpur** (Bhauma-Kara heartland; Varahanatha and the Saptamatrika/Viraja shrines); **Cuttack district** (Simhanath on a river island; Suklesvara); **Puri/Konark** (Jagannath, Konark, and the Prachi valley temples including Varahi at Chaurasi); **Mayurbhanj/Khiching** — the ancient Bhanja capital, where the **Kichakeshwari Temple** (Chamunda; originally 7th–10th c., rebuilt in black chlorite in the 1930s by Maharaja Pratap Chandra Bhanjdeo) is contemporary in style with Brahmesvara/Lingaraja; and the southern extension into **Andhra Pradesh** at **Mukhalingam (Srikakulam)** — the Eastern Ganga capital Kalinganagara — with the Madhukeshwara, Someswara and Bhimeswara Shaiva temples (8th–11th c.) in Kalinga style, plus the Arasavalli sun temple and Srikurmam.

### 11. External influences and comparisons
Kalingan architecture is universally classed as a **regional school of the Nagara** order (Percy Brown, Fergusson, M.A. Dhaky), preserving in some respects a "pure" early Nagara form (its rekha sikhara differs from the Latina/anga-sikhara Nagara of Central India in that the vertical rekha lines originate from the base of the sikhara). Scholars note affinities between the Rajarani gandi and **Khajuraho** (Chandela) clustered-sikhara form. The Khakhara barrel-vault is often compared to the oblong **Dravidian gopuram/shala** roof. The Buddhist **chaitya-window** motif persists as the raha gavaksa; the salabhanjika of Buddhist stupa toranas is the ancestor of the alasa kanya. Claims of direct Southeast Asian influence are best treated as shared inheritance and parallel development rather than demonstrated borrowing — the strongest documented link is the general diffusion of the Surya cult (which UNESCO's Outstanding Universal Value statement traces from 8th-century Kashmir eastward to the shores of Eastern India at Konark).

### 12. Conservation and current state
**Konark** is the focus. The main deul collapsed (causes debated — structural failure of poor khondalite, possible incompletion, iconoclasm attributed by tradition to Kalapahad's 1568 invasion per the Madala Panji, or lightning; several sources place the vimana's final fall in 1837, while Harle and others favor the late 16th century or later), leaving the pidha **Jagamohana**, which British authorities **filled with sand and sealed (1901–1904)** to prevent collapse. UNESCO inscribed the Sun Temple in **1984** (ref. 246; cultural criteria i, iii, vi), describing it as "the culmination of Kalingan temple architecture, with all its defining elements in complete and perfect form." Heavy monsoon rains in **1996–1997** caused the sand-fill and foundation to subside, loose stones to fall into the hollow interior, and cracks from rusting iron clamps (accelerated by sea air); a 2-ton stone reportedly fell from the Jagamohana porch on 19 September 1998. India sought UNESCO Emergency Assistance (1997–98) for a structural stability study; an **ICOMOS reactive monitoring mission** visited in February 2000, after which the Bureau requested help formulating a strategy for **removing the sand**. As of 2026, the ASI — with technical clearance from **IIT Madras** and diamond-core drilling under vibration-free conditions — has begun a cautious scientific evaluation and sand-removal operation on the Jagamohana. Elsewhere, the ASI and Odisha State Archaeology maintain hundreds of living and protected temples; many minor Bhubaneswar shrines survive only partially (some buried to the baranda), and khondalite weathering plus urban encroachment remain chronic threats.

## Recommendations
1. **For authoritative synthesis, start with Donaldson (1985–87) and Panigrahi (1961).** Donaldson's 3-volume *Hindu Temple Art of Orissa* is the single indispensable modern reference (reviewers call it "virtually the only one of its kind"); Panigrahi remains the standard for Bhubaneswar chronology. Use K.S. Behera, *Temples of Orissa* (1993) for a concise overview.
2. **For the theory of design and iconography, use the *Silpa Prakasa*** — ideally the 2005 Baumer/Das edition (more accessible in India than the 1966 Brill) — read alongside Stella Kramrisch, *The Hindu Temple* (for the pan-Indian frame) and N.K. Bose (1932).
3. **Treat dates by tier of certainty.** Cite Brahmesvara (1058/1060) and Ananta Vasudeva (1278) as inscriptionally fixed; present Parasuramesvara (c. 650), Mukteshvara (c. 950–975), Rajarani (c. 1000), Lingaraja (c. 1000–1100), and Konark (c. 1250) as stylistically dated, and always report the range of scholarly opinion (Brown, Fergusson, Panigrahi, Donaldson, Michell) rather than a single date. Flag the Yayati-I attributions (Mukteshvara, Lingaraja) as unproven.
4. **Verify epigraphic claims at the primary level.** For Konark specifically, do not repeat the popular claim that the Kendupatna plate dates the temple; consult Debala Mitra, *Konarak* (ASI, 1968) and Boner/Rath Sarma/Das, *New Light on the Sun Temple of Koṇārka* (1972) for the actual documentary basis.
5. **For conservation research, use the UNESCO SOC dossier (whc.unesco.org, property 246) and ICOMOS mission reports** as primary; supplement with peer-reviewed geological/structural studies on khondalite weathering. Track the 2025–26 ASI sand-removal program through official ASI releases rather than news aggregators.

Benchmarks that would change these recommendations: publication of a critical edition/translation of the *Saudhikagama* (would sharpen *Silpa Prakasa* dating); any newly read foundation inscription for Konark or Lingaraja (would move them from stylistic to fixed dating); or the completion of the ASI/IIT-Madras structural study (would reset the conservation strategy).

## Caveats
- **Dating disputes are real and unresolved** for the marquee temples; this report flags them but cannot adjudicate them. Popular tourism and exam-prep sites frequently state single dates with false precision.
- **Source quality varies sharply.** Wikipedia and government tourism pages were used for orientation and cross-checked against academic works (Donaldson, Panigrahi, Behera, Boner) and primary UNESCO/ICOMOS/ASI documents wherever possible; genealogical and legendary material (e.g., the Dharmapada legend at Konark — the tradition of 1,200 craftsmen led by architect Bishu Maharana over twelve years — and Madala Panji traditions) is tradition, not verified history.
- **The *Silpa Prakasa*'s date and its relation to specific standing temples remain debated** (9th–10th vs. later); its identification of the Varahi temple at Chaurasi as its "Kamagarbha" type is a scholarly hypothesis (Boner), not a certainty.
- **The Kendupatna copper-plate** is commonly miscited; it is a grant of Narasimhadeva II with genealogical content, not a direct construction record for Puri or Konark.
- Some cited figures (e.g., the "700 surviving / 7,000 original" temple counts for Bhubaneswar, and "1,200 workers for 12 years" at Konark) are traditional or estimated figures repeated in the literature, not independently audited counts. There is also a minor internal inconsistency in UNESCO's own materials for Konark (the short description says "six horses," while the OUV synthesis and scholarship say "seven").
